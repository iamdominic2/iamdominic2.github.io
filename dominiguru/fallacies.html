Sunk cost fallacy

(and sometimes it’s not)

Continuing to spend just because you have spent a lot on it.

Eg., casino! Or other useless activities.

It more than likely leads to bankruptcy. Just because you failed 10 times in a row doesn’t change the probability for the next 10 slot plays because machines don’t feel sorry; they want to profit the casino. Casinos straight up abuse such psychology for their own benefit

Or people binge-watch shows or movies until the end because they already paid for it, even if it bores them to the core.

HOWEVER, sometimes, it is indeed better to continue a sunk cost because otherwise you would just give up halfway through everything, and starting over can cost more energy and time relative to something you already started.

In the education system: One ambiguous case is when you take a subject you don't like and later regret. Will you switch subjects or schools in the middle of the year? Or will you have thought it out, since the remaining amount of content is less than if you were to take a new subject? Alternatively, there is an option of redoing the school year, but the opportunity cost is very high. Overall, it may be better to stay, even though the human brain will reject it a bit.

The idea that good receives good and bad receives bad. Not necessarily true. Rather, there is a media bias that only addresses things that are like that, as otherwise the brain would be confused.
There was one psychology experiment of one normal person being electrocuted, and a crowd was unable to intervene. Slowly over time, the crowd falsely thought he had done something really bad.
For instance, a brain that goes through schools will try to think of reasons why it's good! Including adults! Because it's painful to think that bad things can happen for no reason.
And this is the most damaging for things that are totally out of control yet cause suffering, like certain mental or physical health deficits. People try to invent reasons, like the idea that pain builds resilience, due to the cognitive dissonance of seeing that there can be bad without good. In fact, the argument that stress builds resilience is only the case when it is not chronic (i.e. not lasting a whole life!), but the problem is that the people without it are still ahead and don't have to suffer as much.

Conjunction Fallacy

Mathematically, the probability of two things being true at the same time is always less than or equal to the probability of just one of them being true.
Statement 1 (A): He is a writer. Statement 2 (A AND B): He is a writer, AND he studied English in primary school.
Every single person who fits description #2 also fits description #1. But not everyone in #1 fits #2 (some writers might have been homeschooled or studied a different language first). Therefore, the probability of #1 is always higher than or equal to #2.
In logic, more details mean more ways to prove wrong, yet psychologically, a source having more details means a more trustworthy or observant source due to less likelihood of fabrication.

You forgot the first car license plate number you saw today.
But you probably do remember some big good or bad event that changed your life! This comes from the fact that if it is in your mind, it is more difficult to forget and likely more important to you, leading you to notice more when you are aware of a topic. This is expected.
However, the whole reason this is a fallacy is that it leads to overestimation or underestimation of how much you have seen something just by how familiar it looks to you!

Invalid generalization fallacy

This often occurs due to sampling bias, where you apply isolated example(s) to a much larger demographic. For instance, the most common version is trusting what influencers say; for instance, them saying society is stupid and only giving an extremely small number of examples, when most of the people aren’t even involved in his/her argument (this is because they prioritize views and brevity over detailed information due to attention spans)

Another one is when you apply what you have seen to another demographic that has differences. For instance, when you apply what Western philosophies you have heard to Chinese locations, you might think that the Chinese are stupid because they sacrifice individuality. Or you apply what you have heard from teenagers to adult life. This leads to an inconclusive effect and an invalid generalization. To be fair, most people are indeed strangers, and you don’t know much about them. However, DON’T say something like “the whole world is stupid” because that’s just a ridiculous and false overgeneralization.
